# The Allegory

Imagine a group of people stuck in a cave. The only thing they know about the world is the shadows cast on the wall in front of them. No matter how hard they try, they’ll never really know the truth about the objects that cast those shadows — their information is too limited.

In a very real sense, our society, our bodies, our senses, our existence have placed limits on what we can know. The same way the prisoners can only see shadows, we can only learn what we are exposed to. We can’t know the whole truth.

Many of us are prisoners stuck in the cave; we don’t even know that there even _is_ a truth to pursue. _Some of us are prisoners freed from the cave: we know that our ideas are flawed, but at least we can try to find the truth._

---

# The Escaped Prisoner

One of the prisoners leaves the cave and experiences the real world, and then faces the inner conflict of whether he should let the other prisoners experience real life — not knowing if their new lives would be better or worse — or let them live in ignorance in the cave. Since the escaped prisoner’s eyes have become adjusted to daylight, when he returns to the cave he can’t see because it’s too dark. So when he tries to describe the outside world to the prisoners in the cave, they don’t believe him; instead, they see his blindness and think that the outside world is dangerous. It’s implied that anyone who tried to tell the prisoners of the outside world, or removed them from the cave, would be met with hostility or death.

# The Burden of Enlightenment

The moral is that your perception of the world and your personal experiences are not always reality — and that _people would rather live in a world of false perception than change their whole perspective_.

I believe the point is that there is a burden to enlightenment. _It’s not enough to simply experience greater truth; one needs to learn how to present it to others. Trying to “force” people out of ignorance will only cause them to reject the truth more harshly._

# The Platonic Ideal

Picture a chair in your mind. Its color, its texture, whether it has fabric or metal legs — you can summon one instantly.

But you’ve never actually seen _this_ chair. So how is it that I can show you an object you’ve never encountered before, and you can instantly sort it as “chair” or “not chair”? What do all chairs share that every other object lacks — what makes them “chair” and everything else “not chair”?

What we’re doing is constructing an ideal: the “essence of chair” that every real chair has but no real chair perfectly embodies. No one can build this ideal chair; it exists only as a concept we can never fully realize. What we can build are objects that represent “chair.” This essence — the thing we all somehow know but can’t create — has been dubbed the “platonic ideal.”

The cave works the same way. The prisoners never see the real world; they only see its shadows on the wall as it passes the mouth of the cave. The “real world” is the “essence of chair,” and the shadows on our cave wall are the actual chairs you and I know in everyday life.

Shadow puppets are how a lot of people perceive the world, but the philosopher is the freed prisoner — the one who has seen the world as it is and understood it more deeply.

# The Four Acts

The allegory acts as a template for your own change:

1. Your life in the cave. You live in the dark, but are almost entirely unaware of that fact. You accept self-limiting beliefs, the ​[original stories you tell yourself](be-careful-how-you-are-talking-to-yourself.md)​, and mindsets that may not serve you.
2. Your escape from the cave. You question all of the beliefs, mindsets, and stories that were holding you back. You are blinded by the light of the new reality that you now experience. It’s disorienting and scary, but energizing.
3. Your return to the cave. You want to share your excitement about this new reality with others, but find it challenging to do so. You are no longer speaking the same language as the people you used to surround yourself with. You experience a period of loneliness.
4. Your life in the light. You have embraced the vivid reality that is made possible by a growth mindset and limitless beliefs. You find your new people in that reality. You shine in the light.
